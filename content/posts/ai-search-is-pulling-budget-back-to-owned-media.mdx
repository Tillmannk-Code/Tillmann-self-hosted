For most of the last decade, the company website was a brochure. The real work happened somewhere else — on rented platforms, inside algorithms nobody controlled, chasing reach that could be revoked in a product update. The site existed so that people who already knew you could find your pricing.

That arrangement is quietly reversing, and the cause is AI search.

When a meaningful share of discovery runs through language models — assistants summarising, comparing and recommending before a human ever visits a site — the thing being read is no longer a person scrolling a feed. It is a machine ingesting text. And the text it ingests most reliably, most completely, and with the clearest provenance is the text you own. Your website is becoming the corpus. That is a strategic change, not a tactical one, and it should move budget.

## What actually changed

The old funnel assumed a human at every step: see the post, click the link, land on the page, read, convert. Every stage leaked, so reach at the top had to be enormous to matter at the bottom. Social platforms were the rational place to spend, because that is where the reach was.

AI search collapses several of those steps into one. A model reads the available material and produces an answer — often with a recommendation and sometimes with a citation — before the human has expressed a preference at all. The question stops being *how many people saw it* and becomes *what did the model read, and did it read you correctly.*

Those are different questions with different answers, and the second one favours owned media almost entirely. A model can parse a well-structured page. It largely cannot parse a carousel that lived on a platform for three days and then scrolled into oblivion.

## Why social loses relative weight

I want to be careful here, because "social is dead" is the kind of fluent overstatement that gets a consultant quoted and then proven wrong. Social is not dead. It is losing *relative* weight in a specific way.

Most social content is **ephemeral, unstructured, and locked inside platforms that AI crawlers cannot or do not read well.** It does the job of being seen by humans in a moment, then it disappears from the corpus that matters for machine discovery. You rented the attention, the attention passed, and nothing durable was left behind for a model to find later.

The uncomfortable implication is that a large share of social effort now produces **no compounding asset.** It generates in-period reach and no residual. That was always somewhat true; AI search makes it expensive, because the alternative use of that effort — durable, structured, owned content — now pays a second time when machines read it.

## What "boost AI crawlability" actually means

This is where the strategy has to get concrete, because "make your site AI-friendly" is exactly the sort of empty instruction I complain about elsewhere. Crawlability is not a vibe. It is a short list of unglamorous things.

**Let the crawlers in.** Your `robots.txt` decides which AI agents can read the site at all. Blocking them by default — or leaving an old block in place from a nervous quarter two years ago — silently removes you from the corpus. Decide deliberately which bots you welcome.

**Structure the content so a machine can extract it.** Real headings, real prose, answers stated plainly near the question. Clear semantic HTML and schema markup where it fits. The content that gets cited is the content a model can lift a clean, correct sentence out of without guessing.

**Publish the primary sources.** The comparison, the pricing logic, the "how it actually works" explanation, the honest limitations. If you do not state your own facts clearly on a page you own, a model will assemble an answer about you from whatever third-party fragments it can find — and you will not like the average of those.

**Make it fast and static enough to be read.** Content trapped behind interaction, infinite scroll, or heavy client rendering is content a crawler may never see. The most sophisticated page in the world is invisible if it only exists after a click.

None of this is exotic. It is the same discipline as good publishing, aimed at a reader who happens to be a machine.

## The reallocation, concretely

If the argument holds, the money moves. Not all of it, and not overnight — but the direction is clear.

Away from **pure reach on rented platforms**, where the output evaporates. Toward **durable owned content**, where the output is read twice: once by the human who lands on it, and again by every model that ingests it thereafter.

The test I apply to a piece of planned work is simple: *what does this leave behind?* A launch thread that gets ten thousand impressions and then vanishes leaves nothing in the corpus. A clear, structured page answering the question that thread was about leaves an asset that keeps being read. Same effort, wildly different half-life.

Social keeps a real job in this model — but a narrower one. It becomes a **distribution and signal layer**, not the destination. It points humans toward the owned asset and creates the external references that make a model more confident the asset is real. It stops being the thing you build; it becomes the thing that advertises the thing you build.

## The part that is easy to get wrong

Two failure modes, both common.

The first is treating this as a reason to **churn out volume.** AI search rewards durable, structured, *accurate* content — not more content. A hundred thin pages generated to feed the machine will be read, averaged, and correctly identified as thin. The compounding asset is quality that a model can safely cite, not quantity it has to wade through.

The second is **abandoning social entirely** because a strategy deck said owned media won. The reach still matters for the humans who are not routing through an assistant yet, and the external signals still matter for the models that are. The move is reallocation and reweighting, not a bonfire.

## Where this nets out

For ten years the incentives pointed off-site, toward platforms that owned the audience and rented it back to you. AI search points them home. The website is no longer the brochure you send people to after the real marketing happened elsewhere — it is increasingly the primary text that determines how machines describe you to everyone who asks.

That deserves budget it has not had in a decade. Not because owned media is philosophically purer, but because it is now the only place you can put content and know that both the humans and the machines will actually read it. The rented channels gave you reach. The owned channel gives you a corpus. In an AI-mediated market, the corpus is the asset.

Worth checking what yours currently says about you — and whether the crawlers are even allowed to read it.
